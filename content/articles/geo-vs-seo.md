Most businesses need both, in that order. SEO is the foundation, because almost everything that helps AI tools find and quote you also helps Google rank you. GEO is the layer on top: making sure you are named, not just ranked, when someone asks ChatGPT or Google's AI a question.

> **The short version:** keep your SEO, then add GEO. They share most of the groundwork, but ranking well no longer guarantees you get mentioned in AI answers, so you need to check and measure the two separately. How much weight GEO deserves depends on your business and who you're trying to reach.

Every week someone asks us whether they should stop doing SEO and start doing GEO instead. The honest answer is no, and the more useful answer is that the split between them is a strategy question before it is a tactics one.

If you want the full explainer on GEO itself, we've written that up in [What is Generative Engine Optimisation?](/articles/what-is-generative-engine-optimisation). This guide is the practical part: what to do, and in what order.

## What do GEO and SEO have in common?

A fast, well-built site that clearly explains what you do and who you do it for. Content written by people who actually know the subject. Pages that answer real questions. All of that helps you in Google and in AI answers alike.

Google says so directly. Its guidance for AI Overviews and AI Mode is that there are no additional requirements and no special optimisations needed beyond the usual foundations ([Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features)).

## Where do GEO and SEO differ?

Ranking no longer guarantees you'll be mentioned. Ahrefs found that around 38% of URLs cited in AI Overviews also rank in Google's top ten, compared with roughly 76% when it ran the same study in July 2025 ([Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-overview-citations-top-10/)). Ahrefs has refined its method since, so treat the drop as a direction rather than an exact figure. Either way, you can rank well and still be left out of the answer.

| | SEO | GEO |
|---|---|---|
| Where you show up | A list of ten links | One written answer naming a handful of sources |
| What wins | Ranking the page | Being worth quoting |
| Off-site signal | Backlinks | Being mentioned across the web |
| Best content | Thorough pages on a topic | Clear, self-contained answers to one question |
| How you measure it | Rankings, clicks, impressions | Named, linked or absent, per AI tool |
| How it pays off | Clicks to your site | Being recommended, sometimes with a click |

Each AI tool also draws on different sources. In a random sample of 20,000 prompts, Kevin Indig found that 91% of cited URLs appeared in only one of ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google's AI Overviews for the same prompt ([Growth Memo](https://www.growth-memo.com/p/the-consensus-gap)). Being named by one tells you very little about the others, so "AI" is not one channel.

## Do you need both GEO and SEO?

Start with the numbers. For most websites, AI search still sends under 1% of traffic, which Glenn Gabe found when he checked it across the many sites he has access to in June 2025 ([GSQi](https://www.gsqi.com/marketing-blog/ai-search-traffic-compared-to-google/)). It has grown since, and analytics tools don't always recognise a visit that started in an AI tool. But it's a good reason not to rip up a working SEO plan.

Here's how we'd weigh it by type of business.

**Local and trades businesses.** Plumbers, electricians, dentists, accountants. SEO first, especially your Google Business Profile and local service pages. Then GEO, because people are starting to ask AI who the good plumber nearby is. A small business that answers one narrow question well can end up named next to companies it would never outrank.

**D2C and ecommerce brands.** Both, fairly evenly. People increasingly ask AI to compare products and recommend brands, and reviews, press mentions and clear product information feed both channels.

**B2B and professional services.** Lean into GEO sooner. Buyers use AI tools to research and shortlist suppliers before they ever get in touch. If you're not named, you're not on the list.

**Brand new businesses.** SEO basics first. There's no point being quotable until there's something on your site worth quoting.

## What we've seen on our own site

We sell GEO, so we test it on ourselves. We track seven questions a customer might put to AI tools each month. At the end of June 2026 we were named in none of them. By the end of July we were named in three. Every question we showed up for was one we'd written a page about, and every one we missed, we hadn't. Our guide to [checking whether AI engines are naming your business](/articles/are-ai-engines-naming-your-business) walks through the method.

## Where should you start?

You don't need a big budget. This is the order we'd work in:

1. **Check AI can read your site.** Most AI crawlers don't run JavaScript, so content that only loads in the browser looks like a blank page to them. Check your robots.txt isn't blocking AI search crawlers by accident.
2. **Get the SEO basics right.** Clear service pages, an up to date Google Business Profile, and pages that load quickly.
3. **Ask the AI tools yourself.** Put five questions a customer would ask to each tool. Write down whether you're named, and who is instead.
4. **Answer those questions on your site.** One clear page per question, with real facts and named sources. Short and focused beats long and padded.
5. **Get talked about.** Mentions across the web, in press, directories, podcasts and industry sites, now count for more than chasing backlinks.
6. **Measure both, separately.** Rankings and clicks for SEO. Named, linked or absent, per AI tool, for GEO.

**What to skip:** anyone selling you an llms.txt file as an AI visibility fix. Google's own guidance says you don't need AI text files or special markup to appear in its AI features.
